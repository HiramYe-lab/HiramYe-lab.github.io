Chapter 1. Measurable Spaces and Measurable Mappings - Part 1

Posted on 2026-09-29 Edited on 2026-10-07
Lecture notes on Measure Theory (程士宏)

These notes record my self-study of measure theory, both for my own review and to work through the arguments carefully.

Sets and Operations

  • Indicator function

$$ I_A(x)=\begin{cases} 1, &x\in A,\\ 0, &x\notin A. \end{cases} $$

  • Complement: $A^c\dfeq\{ x:x\notin A \}$.

  • Relation between Sets (A is contained in B or B includes A or A is a subset of B) $$ \{x\in A\Rightarrow x\in B\} \Leftrightarrow A\subset B $$

Operations

  • $A\bigcup B\dfeq\{ x:x\in A \text{ or } x\in B \}$. (Union)
  • $A\bigcap B\dfeq \{ x: x\in A \text{ and }x \in B \}$. (Intersection)
  • $A\backslash B\dfeq \{ x: x\in A\text{ and } x\notin B \}$. (Difference)
  • $A\Delta B\dfeq (A\backslash B)\bigcup (B\backslash A)$. (Symmetric Difference)
    • $A\Delta B=B\Delta A$: The region that A and B do not share.

Union and intersection are associative and commutative. They also satisfy the distributive laws: $$ \begin{aligned} \plr{A\bcup B}\bcap C &= \plr{A\bcap C}\bcup \plr{B\bcap C},\\ \plr{A\bcap B}\bcup C &= \plr{A\bcup C}\bcap \plr{B\bcup C}. \end{aligned} $$

The same definitions extend to an arbitrary index set: $$ \begin{aligned} \bcup_{t\in T}A_t&\dfeq\blr{x:\exists t\in T, x\in A_t},\\ \bcap_{t\in T}A_t&\dfeq\blr{x:\forall t\in T, x\in A_t\ }. \end{aligned} $$ In particular, if $A_s\bcap A_t=\varnothing$ for all $s\neq t$ in $T$, the collection $\blr{A_t:t\in T}$ is said to be pairwise disjoint.

Limit

Consider a sequence of sets $\blr{A_n, n\in\mathbb N}$ with $A_n\subset A_{n+1}$. The sequence is then called non-decreasing, written as $A_n\uparrow$, and its limit is $\lim_{n\to \infty}A_n\dfeq \bcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n$. Dually, the sequence of $A_n\supset A_{n+1}$ is called non-increasing, denoted as $A_n\downarrow$, and its limit becomes $\lim_{n\to\infty}A_n\dfeq \bcap_{n=1}^{\infty}A_n$.

Sequences of either kind are called monotone. By definition, every monotone sequence of sets has a limit. Two further limits are the lower and upper limits: $$ \liminf_{n\to\infty} A_n\dfeq \bcup_{n=1}^{\infty}\bcap_{k=n}^{\infty}A_k, \quad\quad\quad \limsup_{n\to\infty}A_n\dfeq \bcap_{n=1}^{\infty}\bcup_{k=n}^{\infty} A_k. $$

  • Lower Limit: The set of elements that appear in all sets from some index $k$.
  • Upper Limit: The set of elements that belong to infinitely many of the sets $A_n$.

Normally, the lower limit is contained in the upper limit, i.e. $\liminf_{n\to\infty} A_n\subset \limsup_{n\to\infty}A_n$. If the two coincide, the limit exists and is given by $$ \lim_{n\to\infty} A_n\dfeq \liminf_{n\to\infty} A_n=\limsup_{n\to\infty} A_n. $$

Collection of Sets (Class of Sets)

A collection of sets is a set where its elements are sets. Some special collections of sets play a significant role as

$\pi$-system

$\mathscr P$ is said to be a $\pi$-system on $X$ if it is closed under intersection: $$ A\bcap B\in \mathscr P,\ \forall A,B\in\mathscr P. $$

  • $\mathscr P_{\mathbb R}\dfeq \blr{(-\infty, a]:a\in\mathbb R}$ is a $\pi$-system on $\mathbb R$.

    Click to expand proof details

    Proof.

    For any $A, B\in\mathscr P$, there exist $a, b\in\mathbb R$ such that $A=(-\infty, a]$ and $B=(-\infty, b]$. Without loss of generality, assume $a \le b$. Then, $A\bcap B=(-\infty, a]\bcap (-\infty, b]=(-\infty, a]=A\in\mathscr P_{\mathbb R}$. Therefore, $\mathscr P_{\mathbb R}$ is a $\pi$-system.

    $\square$

  • $\blr{(a,b):a,b\in\mathbb R}, \blr{(a,b]:a,b\in\mathbb R}, \blr{[a,b):a,b\in\mathbb R},\blr{[a,b]:a,b\in\mathbb R}$ are $\pi$-systems on $\mathbb R$.

    Click to expand proof details

    Proof.

    These collections of sets are denoted as $\mathscr P_1, \mathscr P_2,\mathscr P_3,\mathscr P_4$, respectively.

    In $\mathscr P_1$, $A=(a_1,a_2)\in\mathscr P_1$ and $B=(b_1,b_2)\in\mathscr P_1$. There are several cases to be considered.

    • $b_2\le a_1$ or $b_1\ge a_2$: evidently $A\bcap B=\varnothing\in\mathscr P_1$.
    • $b_2\gt a_1$ and $b_1\lt a_2$: $A\bcap B=\plr{\max(a_1,b_1), \min(a_2,b_2)}\in\mathscr P_1$. In this case the interval is indeed nonempty: since $\max(a_1,b_1)$ is either $a_1$ or $b_1$, it suffices to check both. If $\max(a_1,b_1)=a_1$, then $a_1\lt a_2$ (as $A$ is an open interval) and $a_1\lt b_2$ (by assumption), so $a_1\lt\min(a_2,b_2)$. If $\max(a_1,b_1)=b_1$, then $b_1\lt b_2$ (as $B$ is an open interval) and $b_1\lt a_2$ (by assumption), so $b_1\lt\min(a_2,b_2)$. Hence $\max(a_1,b_1)\lt\min(a_2,b_2)$, and the intersection is a nonempty open interval.

    Therefore, $\mathscr P_1$ is a $\pi$-system.

    The same argument shows that $\mathscr P_2, \mathscr P_3,\mathscr P_4$ are all $\pi$-systems.

    $\square$

Semi-ring

A $\pi$-system $\mathscr L$ is said to be a semi-ring if $\forall A,B\in\mathscr L$ with $A\supset B$, there exist finitely many pairwise disjoint sets $\blr{C_k\in\mathscr L,k=1,\dots,n}$ such that $$ A\backslash B=\bcup_{k=1}^nC_k. $$

  • $\mathscr L_{\mathbb R}\dfeq \blr{(a,b]:a,b\in\mathbb R}$ is a semi-ring on $\mathbb R$.

    Click to expand proof details

    Proof.

    As discussed above, $\mathscr L_{\mathbb R}$ is a $\pi$-system on $\mathbb R$. Assume we have $(a_1,a_2]$ and $(b_1,b_2]$.

    • $b_2\le a_1$ or $a_2\le b_1$: $(a_1,a_2]\backslash (b_1,b_2]=(a_1,a_2]$.
    • $a_1\lt b_2$ and $a_2\gt b_1$:
      • $b_1\le a_1$ and $b_2\le a_2$: $(a_1,a_2]\backslash (b_1,b_2]=(b_2,a_2]$.
      • $b_1\le a_1$ and $b_2>a_2$: $(a_1,a_2]\backslash (b_1,b_2]=\varnothing$.
      • $b_1>a_1$ and $b_2\le a_2$: $(a_1,a_2]\backslash (b_1,b_2]=(a_1,b_1]\bcup (b_2,a_2]$.
      • $b_1>a_1$ and $b_2>a_2$: $(a_1,a_2]\backslash (b_1,b_2]=(a_1,b_1]$.

    Therefore, $\mathscr L_{\mathbb R}$ is a semi-ring.

    $\square$

  • $X=\blr{x_1,\dots,x_n}$ has a finite number of elements. $\mathscr P=\blr{\blr{x_1},\dots,\blr{x_n}}$ is a $\pi$-system and semi-ring.

    Click to expand proof details

    Proof.

    For any $\blr{x_i},\blr{x_j}\in\mathscr P$, $\blr{x_i}\bcap\blr{x_j}$ is $\blr{x_i}$ for $i=j$ and $\varnothing$ otherwise. Therefore, $\mathscr P$ is a $\pi$-system.

    $\forall \blr{x_i},\blr{x_j}\in\mathscr P$, $\blr{x_i}\backslash \blr{x_j}=\blr{x_i}$ for $i\neq j$ and $\varnothing$ for $i=j$; in either case it lies in $\mathscr P$. Thus, $\mathscr P$ is a semi-ring.

    $\square$

Ring

$\mathscr R$ is said to be a ring if it is closed under union and difference: $$ A,B\in\mathscr R\Rightarrow A\bcup B,A\backslash B\in\mathscr R. $$

  • $\mathscr R_{\mathbb R}\dfeq \bcup_{n=1}^{\infty}\blr{\bcup_{k=1}^{n}(a_k,b_k]:a_k,b_k\in\mathbb R}$ is a ring on $\mathbb R$.

    Click to expand proof details

    Proof.

    Any $ A, B\in\mathscr R_{\mathbb R}$ can be rewritten as $A=\bcup_{k=1}^{K}(a_k,b_k]$ and $B=\bcup_{j=1}^{J}(c_j,d_j]$.

    The union of $A$ and $B$ is given by $$ A\bcup B=\plr{ \bcup_{k=1}^{K}(a_k,b_k] } \bcup \plr{\bcup_{j=1}^{J}(c_j,d_j]}, $$ which can also be written by the union of $K+J$ intervals. Therefore, $A\bcup B\in\mathscr R_{\mathbb R}$. The difference is given as $$ \begin{aligned} A\backslash B&=\plr{ \bcup_{k=1}^{K}(a_k,b_k] } \backslash \plr{\bcup_{j=1}^{J}(c_j,d_j]},\\ &= \bcup_{k=1}^{K}\plr{(a_k,b_k] \backslash \plr{\bcup_{j=1}^{J}(c_j,d_j]}},\\ &= \bcup_{k=1}^{K} \plr{\bcap_{j=1}^{J} (a_k,b_k] \backslash (c_j,d_j]}.\\ \end{aligned} $$ Since $\mathscr L_{\mathbb R}$ is a semi-ring, there exist non-overlapping intervals $(x_{k,j,l}, y_{k,j,l}]$ such that $(a_k,b_k]\backslash (c_j,d_j]=\bcup_{l=1}^{L_{k,j}}(x_{k,j,l}, y_{k,j,l}]$, yielding $$ \begin{aligned} A\backslash B&=\bcup_{k=1}^{K} \bcap_{j=1}^{J} \bcup_{l=1}^{L_{k,j}}(x_{k,j,l}, y_{k,j,l}]\\ &=\bcup_{k=1}^{K} \bcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}l_1=1,\dots,L_{k,1}\\l_2=1,\dots,L_{k,2}\\\dots \\ l_J=1,\dots,L_{k,J} \end{subarray}} \plr{ (x_{k,1,l_1}, y_{k,1,l_1}] \bcap (x_{k,2,l_2}, y_{k,2,l_2}]\bcap\dots\bcap (x_{k,J,l_J}, y_{k,J,l_J}] } . \end{aligned} $$ Since $\mathscr L_{\mathbb R}$ is a $\pi$-system, $\plr{ (x_{k,1,l_1}, y_{k,1,l_1}] \bcap (x_{k,2,l_2}, y_{k,2,l_2}]\bcap\dots\bcap (x_{k,J,l_J}, y_{k,J,l_J}] }$ can be rewritten as $(\hat x_{k,l_1,\dots,l_J},\hat y_{k,l_1,\dots,l_J}]$. Therefore, $A\backslash B\in\mathscr R_{\mathbb R}$.

    Hence, $\mathscr R_{\mathbb R}$ is a ring.

    $\square$

  • $X$ is finite and its power set is $\mathscr T$. $\mathscr T$ is a ring. (Obviously)

Field (Algebra)

A $\pi$-system $\mathscr A$ is said to be a field if $X\in\mathscr A$, $A\in\mathscr A\Rightarrow A^c\in\mathscr A$ holds.

It follows from the definitions above that a semi-ring is a $\pi$-system, a ring is a semi-ring, and a field is a ring.

Click to expand proof details

Proof.

  • A semi-ring must be a $\pi$-system. (Obviously)

  • A ring must be a semi-ring.

    • Given a ring $\mathscr R$, it has $A,B\in\mathscr R\Rightarrow A\backslash B, A\bcup B\in\mathscr R$.

      Since $A\backslash B\in\mathscr R$, it remains to show that $\mathscr R$ is a $\pi$-system.

      $A\bcap B=A\backslash\plr{A\backslash B}$. Thus, $A\backslash B\in\mathscr R$, followed by $A\bcap B\in\mathscr R$. Hence, $\mathscr R$ is a $\pi$-system.

      Thus, $\mathscr R$ is a semi-ring.

  • A field must be a ring.

    • Given a field $\mathscr F$, we have $A,B,X\in\mathscr F\Rightarrow A^c,B^c,A\bcap B\in\mathscr F$.

      $A\backslash B=A\bcap B^c\in\mathscr F$ and $A\bcup B=\plr{A^c\bcap B^c}^c\in\mathscr F$ yield that $\mathscr F$ is a ring.

      $\square$

Monotone class

A collection of sets, say $\mathscr M$, is said to be a monotone class if all monotone sequences $\blr{A_n,n=1,2,\dots}$ have a limit in $\mathscr M$.

$\lambda$-system (Dynkin system)

A collection of sets, say $\mathscr L$, is said to be a $\lambda$-system if it satisfies the following conditions:

  • $X\in\mathscr L$
  • $A,B\in\mathscr L, A\supset B\Rightarrow A\backslash B\in\mathscr L$
  • $A_n\in\mathscr L,A_n\uparrow\Rightarrow \bcup_{n=1}^{\infty}A_n\in\mathscr L$

$\sigma$-field ($\sigma$-algebra)

A collection of sets, say $\mathscr F$, is said to be a $\sigma$-field if it satisfies the following conditions:

  • $X\in\mathscr F$
  • $A\in\mathscr F\Rightarrow A^c\in\mathscr F$
  • $A_n\in\mathscr F,n=1,2,\dots\Rightarrow \bcup_{n=1}^{\infty}A_n\in\mathscr F$

The smallest $\sigma$-algebra is $\blr{X,\varnothing}$, and the largest $\sigma$-algebra is $\mathscr T\dfeq \blr{A:A\subset X}$.

It follows from the definitions above that a $\lambda$-system is a monotone class, and a $\sigma$-algebra is a $\lambda$-system.

Click to expand proof details

Proof.

  • A $\lambda$-system is a monotone class

    Given a $\lambda$-system $\mathscr L$, we need to prove all monotone sequences have their limit.

    Assume $\blr{A_n,n=1,2,\dots}$ is a monotonically increasing sequence in $\mathscr L$. Based on the definition of $\lambda$-systems, the limit exists.

    Conversely, given a monotonically decreasing sequence $\blr{B_n,n=1,2,\dots}$, we have $B_n^c\in\mathscr L$ and $\blr{B_n^c, n=1,2,\dots}$ is monotonically increasing so that it has a limit, namely $\bcup_{n=1}^{\infty} B_n^c\in\mathscr L$. Then, $\bcap_{n=1}^{\infty} B_n=\plr{\bcup_{n=1}^{\infty}B_n^c}^c\in\mathscr L$.

    Therefore, $\mathscr L$ is a monotone class.

  • A $\sigma$-algebra is a $\lambda$-system

    Given a $\sigma$-algebra $\mathscr F$, $X\in\mathscr F$ and $A\backslash B=A\bcap B^c=\plr{A^c\bcup B}^c\in\mathscr F$ for $A\supset B$.

    The last condition of $\lambda$-systems is guaranteed easily. Therefore, $\mathscr F$ is a $\lambda$-system.

    $\square$

A measurable space is denoted by $\plr{X,\mathscr F}$, where $\mathscr F$ is a $\sigma$-field on $X$.

A monotone class that is also a field is a $\sigma$-algebra.

Click to expand proof details

Proof.

Given a class $\mathscr M$ that is both a monotone class and a field, we have $X\in\mathscr M$, $A^c\in\mathscr M$ for every $A\in\mathscr M$.

For a sequence of sets $\blr{A_n,n=1,2,\dots}$, define $B_n=\bcup_{k=1}^nA_k$. $\blr{B_n,n=1,2,\dots}$ is a monotonically increasing sequence, and it has its limit, i.e. $\bcup_{n=1}^{\infty} B_n\in\mathscr M$. On the other hand, $\bcup_{n=1}^{\infty} A_n=\bcup_{n=1}^{\infty} B_n$ yields $\bcup_{n=1}^{\infty} A_n\in\mathscr M$.

Therefore, $\mathscr M$ is a $\sigma$-algebra.

$\square$

A $\lambda$-system that is also a $\pi$-system is a $\sigma$-algebra.

Click to expand proof details

Proof.

Given a class $\mathscr L$ that is both a $\lambda$-system and a $\pi$-system, we have $X\in\mathscr L$, $A^c=X\backslash A\in\mathscr L$ for any $A\in\mathscr L$.

For a sequence of sets $\blr{A_n,n=1,2,\dots}$ with $A_n\in\mathscr L$, define $B_n=\bcup_{k=1}^n A_k$ such that $B_n\uparrow$. Then, $\bcup_{n=1}^{\infty} A_n=\bcup_{n=1}^{\infty} B_n\in\mathscr L$.

Therefore, $\mathscr L$ is a $\sigma$-algebra.

$\square$

$\sigma$-ring

A collection of sets $\mathscr R$ is said to be a $\sigma$-ring if $$ \begin{align} A, B\in\mathscr R&\Rightarrow A\backslash B\in\mathscr R,\\ A_n\in\mathscr R,n=1,2,\dots&\Rightarrow \bcup_{n=1}^{\infty}A_n\in\mathscr R. \end{align} $$ Note: A $\sigma$-ring including $X$ is a $\sigma$-field.
